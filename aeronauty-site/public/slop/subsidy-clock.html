The Subsidy Clock: a small annual number wearing a big cumulative coat

Filed 22 June 2026

The specimen. A "Subsidy Clock" (subsidyclock.co.uk, launched via The Daily Sceptic) that totals UK renewable and low-carbon electricity support since 2002 and shows it as a counter ticking upward in real time. The figure quoted on its behalf: £131.8bn in direct subsidies, £227.6bn including indirect, "about £15M per day, or £627k per hour," "paid by every UK energy consumer."

The number is real. The rhetoric around it is the slop.

The flaw, in three parts

1. It never divides. The whole effect depends on letting a quantity run for twenty-four years and then refusing to put a denominator under it. £131.8bn over 24 years is about £5.5bn a year. That is the number the clock will not show you, because £5.5bn is a policy line item and £131.8bn is a headline. "£15M a day" and "£627k an hour" are not new facts, they are the same £5.5bn re-sliced into units small enough to sound frightening. A counter that increments per second dresses a backward-looking accounting total up as live, ongoing spend. It is a calculator with a drum roll.

2. It stuffs the "indirect" total with things that aren't subsidies. The jump from ~£131.8bn to £227.6bn is made by folding in the Capacity Market, carbon pricing (the Climate Change Levy, Carbon Price Support and ETS auction revenue), and transmission and balancing charges. This is not an inference; it is exactly what the site's own methodology page lists under "the indirect bill." Carbon pricing is revenue to the Treasury and a charge on emissions, not a payment to a generator. Transmission and balancing charges are the cost of running any grid, with any mix, including an all-gas one. The site marks every one of these components estimated and concedes the share blamed on renewables is "a stated judgement." Relabelling a tax and general system costs as "renewables subsidy", even a carefully hedged one, is doing the work the direct figure couldn't.

3. The padding is "estimated," by the site's own admission. Here is the honest place to give the clock credit: its direct figure already nets CfD paybacks, so when wholesale runs above the strike price and generators pay money back, the methodology says it counts that. The direct number is not where the inflation lives. The inflation lives in the leap to £227.6bn, which is the "indirect bill", and the site itself marks every component of it estimated, with the attribution a stated judgement rather than a measurement. A number whose own footnotes call it a judgement should not be quoted on LinkedIn as a hard "£227.6Bn."

And the thing the £131.8bn actually bought, guaranteed offtake that drove offshore-wind strike prices down by roughly 70% between 2015 and 2019, is the reason recent CfD auctions clear below wholesale. The subsidy purchased a learning curve. The clock invoices you for the tuition and hides the graduate.

What the clock won't tell you

Timeframe figures are run-rate estimates, exactly as the original Subsidy Clock states: "Every hour" projects the current rate over one hour; "Today" and "This year" apply it to time elapsed since midnight and 1 January. The all-time chip shows the direct figure, then the total with estimated indirect costs in brackets where one exists.

A demonstration, not a hoax. Every headline figure here is real and sourced. The point is that the live-ticking-counter format can be pointed at any large cumulative number, and the ones nobody builds a clock for tend to be the bigger ones. The format is a parody of The Subsidy Clock by Richard Lyon.

The correct framing

Subsidy figures mean nothing as a lump. They mean something per unit of energy and net of value delivered: cost per MWh, against the capacity and generation it financed and the gas imports it displaced, with CfD counted as the two-way contract it is, and with fossil-fuel support, fuel duty freezes and North Sea reliefs, on the same ledger. Do that and the renewables line stops being a scandal and starts being one of the better-value things on the page. Green levies are a small and shrinking slice of a bill that was driven up by gas prices, not by the clock.

Kicker. Any number is a scandal if you let it run for twenty-four years and forbid yourself from dividing. The tell isn't the figure on the Subsidy Clock, it's that nobody ever builds one of these for the bills they'd rather you didn't add up.
