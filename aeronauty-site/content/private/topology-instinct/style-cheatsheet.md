# Style Cheat Sheet — Harry Smith voice for the topology article

Distilled from `Writing Style - Harry Smith.md` and the Aeronauty article planning notes in `Daily/2026-05-01.md`. Reference this every paragraph.

## Voice in one paragraph

Conversational, direct, British. Lead with the point. Contractions always. Parenthetical asides are load-bearing — use them for context, dry humour, side observations. Technical snark is welcome where it lands; the MATLAB rant in the writing-style file is the calibration ("That'll be several thousand of dollars a year, please."). Self-deprecating where genuine, never performatively. Harry has a PhD and twenty years of experience and the article should sound like it. When something is genuinely "it depends," say what it depends on. Profanity sparingly — once or twice across the whole article, where it lands.

## Sentence shape

- **Short, punchy openers** for paragraphs. Don't begin with "In this section we will..." or "It is important to note that..."
- **Mixed sentence length** — paragraphs feel uneven, not metronomic.
- **No throat-clearing**. "Here's the thing..." or "Let's be honest..." are allowed; "I would like to talk about..." is not.
- **Active voice**. "The MILP found a sequence" not "A sequence was found by the MILP."

## Verbs Harry uses

land (an idea lands), drop (it got dropped), cut (the panel cut the project), refuse (I refused to flatten), notice, wire up, point at, miss (I missed it), find, build, ship, push, push back, run, scale, hand off, bridge, surface (a problem surfaces), sit (it sits in a gap), price (the framework couldn't price the work).

## Verbs to avoid

leverage (as a verb), utilise, synergise, drive (as in "drive value"), unlock, deliver (as in "deliver outcomes"), enable (used as filler), ideate, action (as a verb), operationalise.

## Banned phrases

synergy, leverage (verb), touch base, paradigm shift, unlock, journey (as in "my journey"), cutting-edge, revolutionary (when applied to Harry's own work — fine when quoting the mock reviewer who used it), superpower, game-changer, disrupt, "at the end of the day," "moving forward," "moreover," "furthermore," "in conclusion," "as an AI language model," "deep dive," "pain point," "circle back," "low-hanging fruit," "best-in-class."

## British spellings

colour, programme, realise, behaviour, optimised, recognise, organisation, analyse, modelled, signalled, travelled.

## Parentheticals — load-bearing

Use parentheses for:
- Side observations: "(I've seen a propeller wake model written using Excel macros)"
- Disambiguation: "(yes, MATLAB still does this)"
- Dry humour without breaking the sentence: "(Boeing's structure couldn't price that kind of option-value yet)"
- Self-aware caveats: "(I'd drop tools when I was done with them like they ceased to exist)"

If a parenthetical reads like padding, cut it. If a parenthetical does load-bearing work, keep it.

## Snark calibration

The MATLAB rant is the upper bound. Quoted from Harry:

> "Try and do the same line in MATLAB - it will print the current values of all three values and do dick-all about changing their values. Superb."

That cadence is the right calibration. Don't go further. The "Excel for things I really shouldn't" self-deprecation is the lower bound.

## Self-deprecation calibration

Genuine, brief, never performative. Examples that work:
- "I came at MILP from engineering rather than from optimisation, so I missed the tighter formulation."
- "I'd drop tools when I was done with them like they ceased to exist."
- "I have been guilty of using Excel for things I really shouldn't."

What does NOT work: "Just a humble engineer," "I'm no expert but," "perhaps it's just me." Harry has a PhD and twenty years; the article should sound like it.

## Profanity calibration

Cap is twice across the whole article. The ice cream van bellow ("MR ICE CREAM VAN MAN") is one. One use of "fucking" or similar is fine somewhere in beat 4 or beat 8 if it lands. Don't exceed. Don't pepper.

## Hedge language to remove

- "perhaps we could consider"
- "it might be worth"
- "arguably" (almost always; rarely needed)
- "to some extent"
- "in many ways"
- "I think it's fair to say"
- "broadly speaking"

If a sentence reads stronger without the hedge, drop it. If the hedge is doing real epistemic work (Harry is genuinely uncertain), keep it but say what depends on what.

## Anti-LLM-cadence reflexes

If a sentence reads like ChatGPT defaults, rewrite. Specific tells:

- **Tricolons**: "X, Y, and Z" patterns three sentences in a row → break the pattern.
- **"It's important to note that"** → cut the framing, just say the thing.
- **"It's not just X, it's Y"** → overused; vary.
- **Mirror-sentence closing** ("That's the work. That's the through-line. That's the point.") → use sparingly. Once across the whole article max.
- **"Let's unpack this"** → never.
- **"In other words"** → if you need it, the previous sentence was too dense; rewrite that one.

If a beat reads competent but airless, the fix is usually one or two of:
1. A parenthetical aside that adds Harry's actual personality.
2. A British colloquialism replacing a Latinate verb.
3. A short sentence punctuating a long one.

## Phrases that MUST appear verbatim (do not paraphrase)

- "the bridges live in the heads of generalists" — beat 9
- "the brain that was a tax is now an asset" — beat 9
- "I'd drop tools when I was done with them like they ceased to exist" — beat 8
- "I was furious and heartbroken, and it took me a while to realise the system, not the work, was the problem" — beat 4 (after the heartbreak interruption)

## Phrases that should appear (lightly paraphrasable)

- "Paradigm needed an organisation that could absorb cross-domain work, and Boeing's structure couldn't price that kind of option-value yet" — beat 4
- "thanks Jeremy Harris for teaching me that's what I was doing" — beat 5, credit for the pickles-abuse observation
- "the answer was in my subconscious the whole time" — beat 7
- "every fucking day" cadence — only if it lands somewhere; don't force it

## Don't write

- "As an aerospace engineer with twenty years of experience..." — show, don't tell.
- Any sentence that sounds like it could open a corporate blog post.
- "In this article, we'll explore..." — explore is a tell.
- "Stay tuned for the next post" — Aeronauty isn't a series with cliffhangers.
- "Reach out to me on..." — Aeronauty doesn't have a contact CTA.
